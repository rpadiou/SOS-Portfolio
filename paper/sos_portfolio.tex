\begin{abstract}
I ask whether a certified global minimiser of a degree-4 portfolio risk polynomial is worth having.
I solve the order-2 Lasserre relaxation, dense and with correlative sparsity, and certify a point a posteriori when its value matches the bound, to solver accuracy.
On \aN{} synthetic instances, the probability that one local start misses the global minimum grows with the number of assets: in the default regime it is \aPfCalFifteen{} at $n=15$ and \aPfCalFifty{} at $n=50$.
The relaxation certifies all but \aUncert{} of the instances, and only \aCalFiftyCert{} of the default-regime instances at $n=50$.
On 37 US stocks (2015--2025), the certified minimiser lowers volatility and CVaR relative to equal weights, as any risk-minimising rule does.
It is not better than Ledoit--Wolf minimum variance or minimum CVaR on those measures, and no net-return difference is distinguishable from zero.
A Frobenius-ball formulation meant to protect against kurtosis estimation error does not beat the nominal problem, except marginally at the largest noise level; shrinkage towards a structured target does better.
A first version of this work contained errors, which I corrected before running the experiments.
The conclusions hold for one family of synthetic polynomials and one fixed universe.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Portfolio risk that accounts for skewness and kurtosis is a polynomial in the weights.
With the first four moments the objective has degree four and is not convex in general.
A local solver returns a stationary point.
The Lasserre hierarchy \cite{lasserre2001} returns a lower bound, and a feasible point whose value is close to that bound is close to the global optimum, to solver accuracy.
I ask what this certificate is worth on portfolio risk polynomials.

I do three things.
I implement the order-2 relaxation, dense and with correlative sparsity \cite{waki2006}, and a certificate that does not depend on a rank test.
I compare it with multi-start local search on \aN{} synthetic instances (Experiment~A).
I test a Frobenius-ball formulation for estimation error on the kurtosis coefficients (Experiment~B), and the certified minimiser on 37 US stocks (Experiment~C).

Three results stand out.
Local search fails more often than I expected, even on the default synthetic regime.
In the default regime, one start misses the global minimum with probability \aPfCalFifteen{} at $n=15$ and \aPfCalFifty{} at $n=50$.
The certificate is available in most cases, but its cost at $n=50$ exceeds that of 100 local runs.
On real data the certified minimiser differs from the local one on few dates.
It brings no out-of-sample gain over the risk-based baselines.

The first version of this work (tag \texttt{v1.0-paper}) contained errors.
The sparse budget constraint decoupled the cliques, a size-reduction ratio mixed two metrics, a dense timing was unsupported, and a benchmark described as non-convex is convex.
Appendix~\ref{app:v1} lists them with the measured values.
I fixed them before running any experiment below.

\section{Background}\label{sec:bg}
This section defines the finance used below.
Appendix~\ref{app:ind} gives the formula of each indicator as implemented, Appendix~\ref{app:ref} the baseline strategies and Appendix~\ref{app:voc} the vocabulary.

\paragraph{Portfolio, returns, drift.}
A portfolio of $n$ assets is a vector of weights $x\in\R^n$, where $x_i$ is the share of wealth held in asset $i$.
The budget constraint $\mathbf 1^\top x=1$ means that all wealth is invested.
Long-only means $x\ge0$: no short sales.
The simple return of an asset on day $t$ is $r_t=P_t/P_{t-1}-1$; the log return is $\log(P_t/P_{t-1})$.
$P_t$ is the adjusted close, corrected for splits and dividends.
A portfolio earns $x^\top r_t$ in simple returns; log returns do not add up across assets.
The experiments estimate on daily log returns and evaluate on simple returns, as the code does.
Between two rebalancing dates the weights drift: $x_i\leftarrow x_i(1+r_{t,i})/(1+x^\top r_t)$.
Rebalancing is the trade back to the target weights, and it costs money in proportion to what is traded.

\paragraph{Variance, covariance, correlation.}
With $T$ daily returns, $\Sigma_{ij}$ is the sample covariance of assets $i$ and $j$, and $x^\top\Sigma x$ is the variance of the portfolio return.
Volatility is its square root; it is annualised by $\sqrt{252}$, the number of trading days in a year.
The correlation $\Sigma_{ij}/\sqrt{\Sigma_{ii}\Sigma_{jj}}$ lies in $[-1,1]$.
The median pairwise correlation of the \uN{} stocks is \uCorr: diversification lowers risk but does not remove it.

\paragraph{Markowitz.}
Minimum variance solves $\min_x x^\top\Sigma x$ subject to $\mathbf 1^\top x=1$.
Without sign constraints the solution is $\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1/(\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$.
With $x\ge0$ the problem is a convex quadratic program, and any solver returns its global optimum.
Adding a target for the mean return traces the efficient frontier.
Variance does not see asymmetry or tail weight: two portfolios with the same variance can lose very different amounts on their worst days.

\paragraph{Skewness and kurtosis.}
For a return $p$ with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $s$, skewness is $E[(p-\mu)^3]/s^3$ and kurtosis is $E[(p-\mu)^4]/s^4$.
A normal law has skewness 0 and kurtosis 3.
On the \uN{} stocks (\uReturns{} daily log returns) the median skewness is \uSkewMed{}, from \uSkewMin{} to \uSkewMax{}, and it is negative for \uSkewNeg{} stocks.
The median kurtosis is \uKurtMed{}, from \uKurtMin{} to \uKurtMax{}: extreme days are much more frequent than a normal law predicts (fat tails).

\paragraph{Why a polynomial of degree four.}
For $p=x^\top r$ the moment of order $k$ is $E[p^k]=\sum_{i_1,\dots,i_k}x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_k}\,E[r_{i_1}\cdots r_{i_k}]$.
It is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$ in $x$, and its coefficients are the co-moments of the returns.
Variance has degree two, skewness three, kurtosis four.
A risk function that penalises variance and kurtosis and rewards skewness is therefore a polynomial of degree four.
A fourth-order expansion of expected utility is the usual argument for these three terms.
The full fourth co-moment has \uCoMom{} distinct coefficients for \uN{} assets, against \uObs{} observations in a 756-day window.
This is why Experiment~C also uses a sector model with far fewer coefficients.

\paragraph{Three sources of error.}
\emph{Model error}: the polynomial is not the investor's true objective (here it has no return term).
\emph{Estimation error}: its coefficients are estimated from a finite window.
\emph{Optimisation error}: the solver returns a point whose value exceeds the minimum of the polynomial.
The certificate controls only the third.
It bounds $f(\hat x)-\min f$ for the polynomial as given, and says nothing about the first two.
Experiment~A studies the third on synthetic polynomials, Experiment~B the second by drawing errors on the coefficients, and Experiment~C mixes all three.

\section{Risk model}\label{sec:model}
The synthetic objective is
\begin{equation}
f(x)=x^\top\Sigma x+\sum_i\eta_i x_i^3+\sum_{i,j}\kappa_{ij}\,x_i^2x_j^2+\text{concentration terms},
\end{equation}
on $K=\{x\ge 0,\ b_{\mathrm{lo}}\le \mathbf 1^\top x\le b_{\mathrm{hi}}\}$ with $b_{\mathrm{lo}}=0.95$ and $b_{\mathrm{hi}}=1$.
The coefficients $\eta_i$ play the part of skewness and the $\kappa_{ij}$ that of kurtosis.
The last terms are a stylised concentration penalty on the weights.
They are quartic so that $f$ has degree four and they are larger on concentrated positions.
They are not calibrated and not derived from an execution model.
The coefficients $\kappa_{ij}$ are model parameters.
They are not the fourth co-moment tensor of a portfolio, which would be a dense contraction $\sum\kappa_{ijkl}x_ix_jx_kx_l$.
Cross terms exist only within clusters, so the interaction graph is block-structured.
The markets are synthetic and uncalibrated in every regime of Experiment~A.

On market data (Experiment~C) the objective is estimated on a window of 756 days:
$F(x)=\ell_2 m_2(x)-\ell_3 m_3(x)+\ell_4 m_4(x)$, with $m_k(x)$ the sample moment of order $k$ of the centred portfolio return $x^\top\tilde r_t$.
The weights $\ell_k$ are frozen in advance so that each term contributes the same amount on a single-asset portfolio.
There is no return term: the comparison is about risk.
A sector variant keeps only intra-sector co-moments, which is a modelling choice and not a property of the data.

\section{Relaxations and certificates}
The relaxation of order $d$ replaces each monomial $x^\alpha$ with $|\alpha|\le 2d$ by a variable $y_\alpha$.
It asks that the moment matrix $M_d(y)$ and the localising matrices of the constraints be positive semidefinite, and minimises $\sum_\alpha f_\alpha y_\alpha$.
The optimum $\lb$ satisfies $\lb\le f^\star$.
Since $f$ has degree four, $d=2$ is the smallest usable order.
Under an Archimedean condition $\lb$ converges to $f^\star$ as $d$ grows \cite{lasserre2001,putinar1993}.
Convergence at $d=2$ is not guaranteed.

\paragraph{Correlative sparsity.}
Let $I_1,\dots,I_m$ be the maximal cliques of a chordal extension of the interaction graph.
The sparse relaxation uses one moment matrix per clique.
Waki et al.\ \cite{waki2006} establish that it is valid and weaker than the dense one.
Lasserre \cite{lasserre2006} proves that, under the running intersection property, it converges to $f^\star$ as the order grows.
At equal order, $\lb^{\mathrm{sparse}}\le\lb^{\mathrm{dense}}$.
Without the budget variables this is immediate, since each sparse block is a principal submatrix of a dense block.
With the sector-sum variables of the next paragraph, substituting $s_k=\sum_{i\in O_k}x_i$ in a dense moment vector gives a sparse-feasible point with the same objective; a regression test checks it numerically.

The budget $\mathbf 1^\top x\in[b_{\mathrm{lo}},b_{\mathrm{hi}}]$ involves every variable and belongs to no clique.
Version 1 replaced it by a constraint on $E[\sum x]$, which decoupled the cliques.
I now add the sector sums $s_k=\sum_{i\in O_k}x_i$, where $O_k$ is the set of variables owned by clique $k$.
The equalities $s_k=\sum_{i\in O_k}x_i$ live in the clique $I_k\cup\{s_k\}$ and the budget lives in a clique on $\{s_1,\dots,s_m\}$.
This is the star variant.
For many cliques I use partial sums $p_k=p_{k-1}+s_k$, which give cliques of size three (chain variant).
For disjoint cliques, which are the clusters of this paper, both satisfy the running intersection property.
With overlapping cliques the budget clique closes a cycle, the property fails, and the builder warns; the relaxation stays valid.

\paragraph{Certificate.}
After solving, I take candidate points from the Henrion--Lasserre extraction \cite{henrion2005} when the rank condition holds, and from the first-order moments otherwise.
I polish them with a local solve and check feasibility to $10^{-8}$.
A point $\hat x$ is certified when $f(\hat x)-\lb\le\max(10^{-6},10^{-4}|\lb|)$.
Since $\lb\le f^\star\le f(\hat x)$, this holds whatever the rank of the moment matrix.
The reported bound is the primal objective of the moment problem, not the dual objective, so the statement holds to solver accuracy (Appendix~\ref{app:acc}).
The flat-extension test of Curto and Fialkow \cite{curto2005} is reported as a secondary diagnostic.
Solves use CLARABEL \cite{clarabel2024} through CVXPY \cite{cvxpy2016}.
I set its three tolerances (gap absolute, gap relative, feasibility) to $10^{-7}$; its defaults are $10^{-8}$.
The status \texttt{optimal\_inaccurate} means that only its reduced tolerances were met (gap $5\times10^{-5}$, feasibility $10^{-4}$).
The bound is only as accurate as the solver; Appendix~\ref{app:acc} measures the accuracy.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{A two-asset check}\label{sec:two}
The 2-asset benchmark of version 1 has a positive definite Hessian on $K$ (smallest eigenvalue $\tHess$) and a single local minimum.
It validates the code but illustrates nothing about local minima.
I built a non-convex instance by raising the skewness scale until a multi-start run finds two minima (smallest Hessian eigenvalue $\ncHess$).
The best local minimum has value $\ncBestLocal$ and the other $\ncWorstLocal$; \ncShare{} of the local starts end in the worse one.
The order-2 bound is $\ncLb$ and the certificate gap is $\ncGapTwo$.
At $d=3$ the bound is the same to $10^{-8}$, but the rank test fails (ranks $\ncRankDThree$ and $\ncRankDThreePrev$).
So the certificate is available at $d=2$ even though the rank test, applied at a higher order, would reject it.

\subsection{Experiment A: local search against the relaxation}
I generate \aN{} instances with $n\in\{2,4,6,8,10,15,30,50\}$ assets in three regimes, 50 to 120 per cell.
A regime fixes the scale of the three families of coefficients of the risk model.
In the default regime (named ``calibrated'' in the code and the tables, although nothing is calibrated to data) the skewness scale is \rgSkewDef{}, the kurtosis scale \rgKurt{} and the concentration scale \rgImpDef{}.
The mixed regime raises the skewness scale to \rgSkewMix{}.
The stress regime uses skewness scales from \rgSkewStrLo{} to \rgSkewStrHi{}, kurtosis scales from \rgKurtStrLo{} to \rgKurtStrHi{} and concentration scale \rgImpStr{}.
For each instance I solve the sparse relaxation at $d=2$ (the dense one for $n\le10$) and run 100 SLSQP starts drawn i.i.d.\ on the budget set.
The reference optimum $f_{\mathrm{cert}}$ is the certified point when there is one, and the best of 100 runs otherwise.
A search with $K$ starts fails when its best value exceeds $f_{\mathrm{cert}}$ by more than $\max(10^{-6},10^{-4}|f_{\mathrm{cert}}|)$.

\emph{Expected.}
In the default regime I expected local search to find the global minimum, and in the stress regime I expected failures.

\emph{Observed.}
The share of instances with several local minima grows with $n$.
In the default regime it is \aMultiCalFour{} at $n=4$, \aMultiCalFifteen{} at $n=15$ and \aMultiCalFifty{} at $n=50$.

The probability that one start fails grows with $n$ in all regimes (Table~\ref{tab:A}).
In the default regime it is \aPfCalFifteen{} at $n=15$ and \aPfCalFifty{} at $n=50$.
A start fails when it ends above the reference optimum by more than $\max(10^{-6},10^{-4}|f_{\mathrm{cert}}|)$.
Read \aPfCalFifty{} as: of 100 random starts, about \aPfCalFiftyHundred{} end in a worse point (the mean over the 50 instances of that cell).
Lower is better; 0 means that every start finds the optimum.

More starts help without closing the gap (Figure~\ref{fig:A}).
In the default regime at $n=50$, the best of one start fails in \aFOneCalFifty{} of the instances and the best of 100 in \aFHundredCalFifty{}.

\emph{Reference bias.}
On an instance that is not certified, the reference is the best of 100 runs, so 100 starts cannot fail there by construction.
This concerns \aUncert{} instances in total, but \aUncertCalFifty{} of the 50 default-regime instances at $n=50$.
Among the certified ones the 100-start failure rate is \aFHundredCertCalFifty{}, not \aFHundredCalFifty{}.
Table~\ref{tab:A} gives both rates by regime and $n$.
Rates pooled over all instances depend on the design (eight sizes, three regimes, unequal numbers of seeds), so I do not report them.

\emph{Reading.}
The default regime is not an easy regime beyond about ten assets.
I did not test why local minima multiply with $n$.

\subsection{Experiment B: estimation error on the kurtosis coefficients}
The ball formulation minimises $f(x;\hat\kappa)+\delta\,\|Wz(x)\|_2$, where $z(x)$ stacks the monomials $x_i^4$ and $x_i^2x_j^2$ and $W$ weights the off-diagonal entries by $\sqrt2$.
It is the worst case over a Frobenius ball of radius $\delta$ around $\hat\kappa$.
It is parametric robustness; it is not the moment-uncertainty set of Delage and Ye \cite{delage2010}.
I draw four markets ($n=6,10$; mixed and stress regimes).
I perturb the true coefficient vector $\theta$ with an error $\varepsilon$ of fixed relative size $\rho=\|\varepsilon\|_W/\|\theta\|_W\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5\}$, with \bDraws{} draws per market and $\rho$: at $\rho=0.5$ the estimate is off by half the size of the truth.
I compare four methods.
The nominal problem has $\delta=0$.
The ball uses $\delta=\gamma\|\theta\|_W$ for six values of $\gamma$.
The automatic rule sets $\delta=c\hat\sigma$ from a bootstrap of $T=20$ synthetic observations.
Shrinkage with intensity $s\in[0,1]$ replaces $\hat\kappa$ by $(1-s)\hat\kappa+s\,\kappa_{\mathrm{target}}$, where the target has one diagonal value and one off-diagonal value, each the mean of the estimates.
The metric is the true regret $f(\hat x;\kappa)-f(x^\star;\kappa)$: the extra risk, in the units of $f$, that the portfolio chosen with the noisy coefficients carries when judged with the true ones.
It is never negative; smaller is better, and a ratio to the nominal regret below 1 means the method helps.

\emph{Expected.}
I expected the ball to lower the regret when $\rho$ is large and to cost little when $\rho$ is small.

\emph{Observed.}
On the 10-asset mixed market the ball is worse than nominal for $\rho\le0.2$, and the loss grows with $\delta$ (Figure~\ref{fig:B}, Table~\ref{tab:B}).
At $\rho=0.05$ the mean regret is \bTenANom{} for nominal and \bTenARob{} for $\gamma=0.1$, that is, the ball multiplies the regret by \bRatioRobA{}.

At $\rho=0.5$ the ball with $\gamma\le0.1$ lowers the mean regret by 2 to 5\%, and the bootstrap intervals exclude zero.
At $\gamma=0.2$ the difference is not distinguishable from zero, and with $\gamma\ge0.5$ the ball is worse again.
Shrinkage with $s=0.75$ brings the regret to \bRatioShrC{} of the nominal value.
The automatic rule never beats nominal.

In the stress markets the nominal solution is a corner that coincides with $x^\star$ in at least \bStressMin{} of the draws.
One cell discriminates: 10 assets at $\rho=0.5$, where nominal regret is \bStressNominal{}.
There the ball with $\gamma=1$ raises it to \bStrBig{}, and shrinkage with $s=0.5$ brings it to \bStrShr{}.

An order-2 SDP check on \bSdpN{} solves certifies the nominal local result in \bSdpNom{} of the cases.
It certifies the ball results in \bSdpGSmall{} of the cases for $\gamma=0.02$ and \bSdpGBig{} for $\gamma=1$.

\emph{Reading.}
The ball adds a penalty proportional to $\delta$, and here the nominal regret is already small.
Shrinkage helps more, probably because the synthetic coefficients are drawn i.i.d.\ from narrow ranges, which makes the mean-based target close to the truth.
I did not test this explanation, and the markets are synthetic.

\subsection{Experiment C: real data}\label{sec:C}
The data are Yahoo Finance adjusted closes for 37 large US stocks in 9 sectors of the GICS classification, 2009--2025, fixed ex post.
The list is today's large caps, so stocks that fell out of the index are absent: this is survivorship bias, and it flatters every strategy.
The study is walk-forward.
On the first trading day of each month from 2015 to 2025 (\cDates{} dates) each strategy estimates its inputs on the previous 756 trading days, about three years, and sets weights.
The weights then drift until the next date.
Weights are long-only with a cap of 0.25, so that no stock exceeds a quarter of the portfolio; the cap was chosen for the conditioning of the SDP, not for finance (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
It applies to the minimum-variance, minimum-CVaR and polynomial strategies; 1/N, ERC and HRP are not capped.
Costs are 5 or 10 basis points (1 bp $=10^{-4}$) per unit of traded notional; Appendix~\ref{app:ind} defines turnover.
Universe U10 has ten stocks, the first two of each of the first five sectors, and the full (dense) polynomial model; the protocol calls this variant V1.
Universe U37 has all 37 stocks and the sector model, which keeps only co-moments between stocks of the same sector (variant V2).
In the model $F=\ell_2m_2-\ell_3m_3+\ell_4m_4$ of Section~\ref{sec:model}, the scale $\ell_k=w_k/\overline{|m_k(e_i)|}$ makes each term worth $w_k$ on a portfolio holding a single stock $e_i$, on average over the stocks.
I fix $(w_2,w_3)=(1,1)$ and let $w_4\in\{0,1,3\}$ be the weight of the fourth moment; $w_4=1$ is the reference value fixed before the runs.
Returns are first rescaled so that the average stock volatility is one.
The protocol was frozen before any run, with three amendments made before the evaluation period (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
Indicators are defined once in Appendix~\ref{app:ind}; the rule for reading each is given where the result appears.
Comparators are equal weights, Ledoit--Wolf minimum variance \cite{ledoit2004}, equal risk contribution (ERC), hierarchical risk parity (HRP) \cite{lopezdeprado2016} and minimum CVaR \cite{rockafellar2000}; Appendix~\ref{app:ref} says what each optimises.

\emph{Expected.}
Third and fourth sample co-moments are noisy on 756 days.
I expected the global and local minimisers to coincide most of the time, and neither to be distinguishable from simple baselines \cite{demiguel2009}.

\emph{Observed.}
On U10 the certified minimiser equals the local one on every date.
On U37 the local solver is suboptimal by more than $10^{-4}$ in relative terms on \cSubZero{}, \cSubOne{} and \cSubThree{} of the dates, for $w_4=0,1,3$.
Every SOS solve is certified.

Out of sample the picture is flat (Table~\ref{tab:C}, Figure~\ref{fig:C}).
The net return of a strategy is its mean daily return after costs, times 252; higher is better.
Across the \mNStrat{} strategies of U37 it runs from \mNetMin{}\% to \mNetMax{}\% a year, and equal weights earn \mNetEw{}\%.
A difference between two strategies comes with a 95\% interval from a block bootstrap (Appendix~\ref{app:voc}); if the interval contains zero, the data do not separate the two.
All \cTests{} net-return intervals contain zero.
For U10 against equal weights, the net-return difference is \cTenEwNet{} points a year, with interval \cTenEwNetLo{} to \cTenEwNetHi{}, on a base of \mTenNetEw{}\%.
The Sharpe-ratio test gives $p$ between \cPMin{} and \cPMax{} (Appendix~\ref{app:tests}, uncorrected); a $p$ above 0.05 means that a difference this large would arise by chance more than one time in twenty if the two Sharpe ratios were equal.

On risk, the picture depends on the baseline.
Volatility is the annualised standard deviation of daily returns, and CVaR is the mean loss on the worst 5\% of days; for both, lower is better.
Equal weights have a volatility of \mTenVolEw{}\% on U10, so a difference of one point is about \mTenVolOnePt{}\% of the base.
With $w_4=1$ the U10 model has lower volatility than equal weights, by \cTenEwVol{} points (interval \cTenEwVolLo{} to \cTenEwVolHi{}).
It has higher volatility and CVaR than minimum variance and minimum CVaR.

On U37 the sector model is not better than the local solution of the same model.
Its volatility differs by \cSecLocVol{} points (\cSecLocVolLo{} to \cSecLocVolHi{}), so it is slightly higher.

The price of sparsity is large in model terms.
The full-model objective at the sector solution is \cSpSecOne{} on average against \cSpFullOne{} at the full-model local solution ($w_4=1$).
It is worse on \cSpWorseOne{} of the dates.
The objective is dimensionless (each term is scaled by $\ell_k$), so only the ratio between the two means is informative.

Time is the wall-clock time per monthly rebalance.
The SOS solve takes \cTimeTen{} s per rebalance on U10 and \cTimeSector{} s on U37, against \cTimeLocal{} s for the local solve of the sector model.
These are wall times with six concurrent workers and are indicative only.

\emph{Reading.}
The sparse global minimiser is better at the thing it optimises, and the gain does not transfer.
The most plausible reason is estimation error in the moments, which is what the protocol anticipated.
I did not separate it from model error.

\section{Discussion and limits}
What the experiments show.
On synthetic polynomials, local search fails often enough that a certificate has value, especially above ten assets.
The order-2 relaxation produces it in most cases, with a cost that exceeds 100 local runs at $n=50$.
On real data the certificate changes the allocation on few dates and does not improve on the risk-based baselines.

What they do not show.
They do not show that the certificate is useless for other objectives.
They do not show that the ball formulation fails for other uncertainty models, or with real estimation error instead of drawn error.
They do not show anything about markets beyond 37 fixed US stocks over one period.
The extrapolated dense costs at $n=30$ and $n=50$ are not measurements.

The bound-accuracy test covers validity and tightness at the $10^{-8}$ level on small non-convex instances with two or three assets.
It does not cover four assets or more, sparse relaxations with several cliques and auxiliary budget variables, or the real-data polynomials.
For those I rely on the re-solve sample of Appendix~\ref{app:acc}, which has no independent optimum, so a bias common to all solvers would go undetected.

Explanations I tested: the budget decoupling (fixed and confirmed by the regression test), and the solver accuracy behind the bound excess (Appendix~\ref{app:acc}).
Explanations I did not test: why local minima multiply with $n$, why shrinkage beats the ball in Experiment~B, and why the global minimiser does not transfer out of sample.
The synthetic polynomial has no return term and a simplified kurtosis structure, so Experiments~A and~B describe this family only.

Parts of the code and the text were written with an AI assistant.
I reviewed the results, ran an audit of the claims, and corrected the errors it found (Appendix~\ref{app:v1} and \texttt{docs/CLAIMS.md}).

\section{Accuracy of the bound}\label{app:acc}
In Experiment~C the bound exceeds the value of the same polynomial at the retained point, by a relative amount up to \xDefMaxU{}.
The sign is always the same, which first looked like an invalid relaxation.
I re-solved \xN{} windows with CLARABEL tolerance $10^{-9}$ instead of $10^{-7}$.
Where the solver converges (no weight on the fourth moment, and the 2020 windows of U37) the excess falls by a factor 30 to 100.
Where it stalls with an inaccurate status (most cases with $w_4\ge1$) the median ratio of the two excesses is 1.00, and the excess stays between $10^{-7}$ and $1.5\times10^{-6}$ in most cases.
The largest excess at tolerance $10^{-9}$ is \xTightMax{} and none is negative.
The polynomial is the same on both sides (checked to nine digits) and the retained points are feasible (largest violation $4\times10^{-16}$).
The reported value is the primal objective of the moment problem; the guaranteed bound would be the dual objective, which I did not extract.
On a two-asset instance with a known optimum the excess is $3.2\times10^{-9}$ and the solver residuals are about $10^{-9}$, at tolerance $10^{-7}$ and $10^{-10}$ alike.
I conclude that the excess is a precision floor of the solver, consistent with an unfinished solve, and not a flaw in the relaxation.

I re-solved a fixed sample of \spCases{} cases (seed 2026) with CLARABEL at $10^{-10}$ and SCS at $10^{-8}$ (Table~\ref{tab:F}).
The sample includes inaccurate statuses, uncertified instances and the tightest certificate gaps of each experiment.
Across the \spRuns{} re-solves, \spFlips{} certification verdicts change and \spFlagCh{} failure flags of Experiment~A change.

SCS differs by up to \spAScs{} on the 4-asset instances of A, where it also reports an inaccurate status.

\paragraph{Independent optimum.}
A test (\texttt{tests/test\_bound\_soundness.py}) compares the bound with a minimum computed without the SDP on \smN{} instances with two or three assets, by a fine grid followed by local refinement.
It also checks that the bound stays below $f$ at 2000 random feasible points, at the local solution and at the extracted points.
At tolerance $10^{-10}$ the difference between the bound and the independent minimum lies between \smMin{} and \smMax{}.
A tolerance of $10^{-9}$ cannot be enforced because the solver stalls with residuals of that order.
The test therefore allows $+10^{-8}$ (bound above the optimum) and $-5\times10^{-8}$ (bound far below it).

\section{Comparison strategies}\label{app:ref}
All five use returns only, as the polynomial does, and none forecasts the mean.
They span the range from no estimation to tail-risk optimisation.
\paragraph{Equal weights (1/N).}
Each of the $n$ stocks gets $1/n$.
It optimises nothing and assumes nothing about the moments.
It is the reference of DeMiguel et al.\ \cite{demiguel2009}: an estimated rule that cannot beat it has no use for its estimates.
\paragraph{Minimum variance with Ledoit--Wolf.}
It minimises $x^\top\hat\Sigma x$ on the long-only budget set with the cap, where $\hat\Sigma$ is the Ledoit--Wolf estimate.
Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage averages the sample covariance with a multiple of the identity, with an intensity computed from the data to cut estimation error \cite{ledoit2004}.
It assumes that variance is the risk.
It is the reference for the second-moment part of the polynomial.
\paragraph{Equal risk contribution (ERC).}
It sets weights so that each stock contributes the same share of portfolio variance, $x_i(\hat\Sigma x)_i$ equal across $i$.
The code solves $\min\ \tfrac12y^\top\hat\Sigma y-\tfrac1n\sum_i\log y_i$ and normalises $y$.
It lies between equal weights and minimum variance, and it has no cap.
\paragraph{Hierarchical risk parity (HRP).}
It follows L\'opez de Prado \cite{lopezdeprado2016}.
It clusters the stocks on the distance $\sqrt{(1-\mathrm{corr}_{ij})/2}$ with single linkage, orders them by the dendrogram, then splits the list in two and gives each half a share inversely proportional to its variance, recursively.
It needs no matrix inversion, uses the sample covariance and has no cap.
It serves as a reference that is robust to estimation error.
\paragraph{Minimum CVaR.}
It minimises the mean loss on the worst 5\% of the 756 days of the window, as the linear program of Rockafellar and Uryasev \cite{rockafellar2000}: minimise $z+\frac1{(1-0.95)T}\sum_tu_t$ with $u_t\ge-r_t^\top x-z$, $u_t\ge0$, on the capped budget set.
It uses the empirical distribution and no model.
It is the closest competitor to a tail-risk polynomial, since both target the tails.

\section{Vocabulary}\label{app:voc}
\paragraph{Statistics.}
\emph{In-sample} is the window used to estimate; \emph{out-of-sample} is the later period on which the result is judged.
A \emph{backtest} replays a strategy on past data.
\emph{Look-ahead} is the use of information not available at the decision date; here each window ends the day before the rebalance.
\emph{Overfitting} is a rule that fits the noise of its estimation window.
\emph{Survivorship bias} is the absence of stocks that failed.
A \emph{rolling window} moves forward with the decision date.
The \emph{95\% interval} of a difference is the range from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of the difference over 2000 resamples of the evaluation days.
The resampling is a \emph{circular block bootstrap}: it draws blocks of 21 consecutive days, to keep the dependence between neighbouring days.
A \emph{$p$-value} is the probability, if the null hypothesis were true, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed; the Sharpe test uses blocks of 5 days and 2000 resamples.
With \emph{multiple comparisons}, the chance that some interval excludes zero grows with their number.
For \cTests{} independent comparisons at 5\% one expects \cExpectedChance{} such intervals by chance alone; the comparisons here are dependent, and no correction is applied.
\paragraph{Optimisation.}
A \emph{local minimum} is a point no feasible neighbour improves; a \emph{global minimum} is the best feasible point.
A local solver stops at the first local minimum it reaches.
\emph{Multi-start} runs it from many random points and keeps the best; it gives no proof.
A \emph{relaxation} replaces the problem by an easier one whose feasible set is larger, so its optimum $\lb$ is a lower \emph{bound}: no feasible point has a smaller value.
A \emph{certificate} is a feasible point $\hat x$ with $f(\hat x)-\lb\le\max(10^{-6},10^{-4}|\lb|)$.
Then $\hat x$ is $\varepsilon$-optimal: within $\varepsilon$ of the global minimum, $\varepsilon$ being that tolerance.
The \emph{solver tolerance} is the accuracy at which an interior-point method stops; such a method follows a path through the interior of the feasible cone.
``Certified'' therefore means certified to the precision of the solver (Appendix~\ref{app:acc}), not exactly.
\emph{SDP} (semidefinite program) is optimisation over matrices constrained to be positive semidefinite.
