\begin{abstract}
I ask whether a certified global minimiser of a degree-4 portfolio risk polynomial is worth having.
I solve the order-2 Lasserre relaxation, dense and with correlative sparsity, and certify a point a posteriori when its value matches the bound.
On \aN{} synthetic instances, one local start misses the global minimum in \aFailOne{} of the cases, and the relaxation certifies \aCert{} of them.
On 37 US stocks (2015--2025), the certified minimiser lowers volatility and CVaR relative to equal weights, as any risk-minimising rule does.
It is not better than Ledoit--Wolf minimum variance or minimum CVaR on those measures, and no net-return difference is distinguishable from zero.
A Frobenius-ball formulation meant to protect against kurtosis estimation error does not beat the nominal problem, except marginally at the largest noise level; shrinkage towards a structured target does better.
A first version of this work contained errors, which I corrected before running the experiments.
The conclusions hold for one family of synthetic polynomials and one fixed universe.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Portfolio risk that accounts for skewness and kurtosis is a polynomial in the weights.
With the first four moments the objective has degree four and is not convex in general.
A local solver returns a stationary point.
The Lasserre hierarchy \cite{lasserre2001} returns a lower bound, and a feasible point whose value is close to that bound is globally optimal.
I ask what this certificate is worth on portfolio risk polynomials.

I do three things.
I implement the order-2 relaxation, dense and with correlative sparsity \cite{waki2006}, and a certificate that does not depend on a rank test.
I compare it with multi-start local search on \aN{} synthetic instances (Experiment~A).
I test a Frobenius-ball formulation for estimation error on the kurtosis coefficients (Experiment~B), and the certified minimiser on 37 US stocks (Experiment~C).

Three results stand out.
Local search fails more often than I expected, even on the default synthetic regime.
With one start it misses the global minimum in \aFailOne{} of the instances, and in \aBigFailOne{} of those with $n\ge 30$ assets.
The certificate is available in most cases, but its cost at $n=50$ exceeds that of 100 local runs.
On real data the certified minimiser differs from the local one on few dates.
It brings no out-of-sample gain over the risk-based baselines.

The first version of this work (tag \texttt{v1.0-paper}) contained errors.
The sparse budget constraint decoupled the cliques, a size-reduction ratio mixed two metrics, a dense timing was unsupported, and a benchmark described as non-convex is convex.
Appendix~\ref{app:v1} lists them with the measured values.
I fixed them before running any experiment below.

\section{Risk model}
The synthetic objective is
\begin{equation}
f(x)=x^\top\Sigma x+\sum_i\eta_i x_i^3+\sum_{i,j}\kappa_{ij}\,x_i^2x_j^2+\text{concentration terms},
\end{equation}
on $K=\{x\ge 0,\ b_{\mathrm{lo}}\le \mathbf 1^\top x\le b_{\mathrm{hi}}\}$ with $b_{\mathrm{lo}}=0.95$ and $b_{\mathrm{hi}}=1$.
The last terms are a stylised concentration penalty on the weights.
They are quartic so that $f$ has degree four and they are larger on concentrated positions.
They are not calibrated and not derived from an execution model.
The coefficients $\kappa_{ij}$ are model parameters.
They are not the fourth co-moment tensor of a portfolio, which would be a dense contraction $\sum\kappa_{ijkl}x_ix_jx_kx_l$.
Cross terms exist only within clusters, so the interaction graph is block-structured.
The markets are synthetic and uncalibrated in every regime of Experiment~A.

On market data (Experiment~C) the objective is estimated on a window of 756 days:
$F(x)=\ell_2 m_2(x)-\ell_3 m_3(x)+\ell_4 m_4(x)$, with $m_k(x)$ the sample moment of order $k$ of the centred portfolio return $x^\top\tilde r_t$.
The weights $\ell_k$ are frozen in advance so that each term contributes the same amount on a single-asset portfolio.
There is no return term: the comparison is about risk.
A sector variant keeps only intra-sector co-moments, which is a modelling choice and not a property of the data.

\section{Relaxations and certificates}
The relaxation of order $d$ replaces each monomial $x^\alpha$ with $|\alpha|\le 2d$ by a variable $y_\alpha$.
It asks that the moment matrix $M_d(y)$ and the localising matrices of the constraints be positive semidefinite, and minimises $\sum_\alpha f_\alpha y_\alpha$.
The optimum $\lb$ satisfies $\lb\le f^\star$.
Since $f$ has degree four, $d=2$ is the smallest usable order.
Under an Archimedean condition $\lb$ converges to $f^\star$ as $d$ grows \cite{lasserre2001,putinar1993}.
Convergence at $d=2$ is not guaranteed.

\paragraph{Correlative sparsity.}
Let $I_1,\dots,I_m$ be the maximal cliques of a chordal extension of the interaction graph.
The sparse relaxation uses one moment matrix per clique \cite{waki2006,lasserre2006}.
Every sparse constraint is implied by the dense ones at the same order, so I expect $\lb^{\mathrm{sparse}}\le\lb^{\mathrm{dense}}$, and I test it.

The budget $\mathbf 1^\top x\in[b_{\mathrm{lo}},b_{\mathrm{hi}}]$ involves every variable and belongs to no clique.
Version 1 replaced it by a constraint on $E[\sum x]$, which decoupled the cliques.
I now add the sector sums $s_k=\sum_{i\in O_k}x_i$, where $O_k$ is the set of variables owned by clique $k$.
The equalities $s_k=\sum_{i\in O_k}x_i$ live in the clique $I_k\cup\{s_k\}$ and the budget lives in a clique on $\{s_1,\dots,s_m\}$.
This is the star variant.
For many cliques I use partial sums $p_k=p_{k-1}+s_k$, which give cliques of size three (chain variant).
Both satisfy the running intersection property.

\paragraph{Certificate.}
After solving, I take candidate points from the Henrion--Lasserre extraction \cite{henrion2005} when the rank condition holds, and from the first-order moments otherwise.
I polish them with a local solve and check feasibility to $10^{-8}$.
A point $\hat x$ is certified when $f(\hat x)-\lb\le\max(10^{-6},10^{-4}|\lb|)$.
Since $\lb\le f^\star\le f(\hat x)$, this holds whatever the rank of the moment matrix.
The flat-extension test of Curto and Fialkow \cite{curto2005} is reported as a secondary diagnostic.
Solves use CLARABEL \cite{clarabel2024} through CVXPY \cite{cvxpy2016} with tolerance $10^{-7}$.
The bound is only as accurate as the solver; Appendix~\ref{app:acc} measures the accuracy.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{A two-asset check}\label{sec:two}
The 2-asset benchmark of version 1 has a positive definite Hessian on $K$ (smallest eigenvalue $\tHess$) and a single local minimum.
It validates the code but illustrates nothing about local minima.
I built a non-convex instance by raising the skewness scale until a multi-start run finds two minima (smallest Hessian eigenvalue $\ncHess$).
The best local minimum has value $\ncBestLocal$ and the other $\ncWorstLocal$; \ncShare{} of the local starts end in the worse one.
The order-2 bound is $\ncLb$ and the certificate gap is $\ncGapTwo$.
At $d=3$ the bound is the same to $10^{-8}$, but the rank test fails (ranks $\ncRankDThree$ and $\ncRankDThreePrev$).
So the certificate is available at $d=2$ even though the rank test, applied at a higher order, would reject it.

\subsection{Experiment A: local search against the relaxation}
I generate \aN{} instances with $n\in\{2,4,6,8,10,15,30,50\}$ assets in three regimes (default, mixed, stress), 50 to 120 per cell.
For each instance I solve the sparse relaxation at $d=2$ (the dense one for $n\le10$) and run 100 SLSQP starts drawn i.i.d.\ on the budget set.
The reference optimum $f_{\mathrm{cert}}$ is the certified point when there is one, and the best of 100 runs otherwise.
A search with $K$ starts fails when its best value exceeds $f_{\mathrm{cert}}$ by more than $\max(10^{-6},10^{-4}|f_{\mathrm{cert}}|)$.

\emph{Expected.}
In the default regime I expected local search to find the global minimum, and in the stress regime I expected failures.

\emph{Observed.}
Several local minima appear in \aMultiCal{}, \aMultiMix{} and \aMultiStr{} of the default, mixed and stress instances.

The probability that one start fails grows with $n$ in all regimes (Table~\ref{tab:A}).
In the default regime it is \aPfCalFifteen{} at $n=15$ and \aPfCalFifty{} at $n=50$.

More starts help without closing the gap (Figure~\ref{fig:A}).
Over all instances the best of one start fails in \aFailOne{} of the cases and the best of 100 in \aFailHundred{}.
In the default regime at $n=50$, 100 starts still fail in \aCalFiftyFailHundred{} of the instances.

\emph{Reading.}
The default regime is not an easy regime beyond about ten assets.
I did not test why local minima multiply with $n$.

\subsection{Experiment B: estimation error on the kurtosis coefficients}
The ball formulation minimises $f(x;\hat\kappa)+\delta\,\|Wz(x)\|_2$, where $z(x)$ stacks the monomials $x_i^4$ and $x_i^2x_j^2$ and $W$ weights the off-diagonal entries by $\sqrt2$.
It is the worst case over a Frobenius ball of radius $\delta$ around $\hat\kappa$.
It is parametric robustness; it is not the moment-uncertainty set of Delage and Ye \cite{delage2010}.
I draw four markets ($n=6,10$; mixed and stress regimes).
I perturb the true coefficient vector with an error of relative size $\rho\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5\}$, with \bDraws{} draws per market and $\rho$.
I compare four methods.
The nominal problem has $\delta=0$.
The ball uses $\delta=\gamma\|\theta\|_W$ for six values of $\gamma$.
The automatic rule sets $\delta=c\hat\sigma$ from a bootstrap of $T=20$ synthetic observations.
Shrinkage moves $\hat\kappa$ towards a structured target.
The metric is the true regret $f(\hat x;\kappa)-f(x^\star;\kappa)$.

\emph{Expected.}
I expected the ball to lower the regret when $\rho$ is large and to cost little when $\rho$ is small.

\emph{Observed.}
On the 10-asset mixed market the ball is worse than nominal for $\rho\le0.2$, and the loss grows with $\delta$ (Figure~\ref{fig:B}, Table~\ref{tab:B}).
At $\rho=0.05$ the mean regret is \bTenANom{} for nominal and \bTenARob{} for $\gamma=0.1$.

At $\rho=0.5$ the ball with $\gamma\le0.1$ lowers the mean regret by 2 to 5\%, and the bootstrap intervals exclude zero.
At $\gamma=0.2$ the difference is not distinguishable from zero, and with $\gamma\ge0.5$ the ball is worse again.
Shrinkage with $s=0.75$ brings the regret to \bRatioShrC{} of the nominal value.
The automatic rule never beats nominal.

In the stress markets the nominal solution is a corner that coincides with $x^\star$ in at least \bStressMin{} of the draws.
One cell discriminates: 10 assets at $\rho=0.5$, where nominal regret is \bStressNominal{}.
There the ball with $\gamma=1$ raises it to \bStrBig{}, and shrinkage with $s=0.5$ brings it to \bStrShr{}.

An order-2 SDP check on \bSdpN{} solves certifies the nominal local result in \bSdpNom{} of the cases.
It certifies the ball results in \bSdpGSmall{} of the cases for $\gamma=0.02$ and \bSdpGBig{} for $\gamma=1$.

\emph{Reading.}
The ball adds a penalty proportional to $\delta$, and here the nominal regret is already small.
Shrinkage helps more, probably because the synthetic coefficients are drawn i.i.d.\ from narrow ranges, which makes the mean-based target close to the truth.
I did not test this explanation, and the markets are synthetic.

\subsection{Experiment C: real data}\label{sec:C}
The data are Yahoo Finance adjusted closes for 37 large US stocks in 9 sectors, 2009--2025, fixed ex post.
This introduces survivorship bias.
I rebalance monthly from 2015 to 2025 (\cDates{} dates) with a 756-day window, long-only weights capped at 0.25, and costs of 5 and 10 bp.
Universe U10 has ten assets and the dense model.
Universe U37 has all 37 assets and the sector model.
The protocol was frozen before any run, with three amendments made before the evaluation period (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
Comparators are equal weights, Ledoit--Wolf minimum variance \cite{ledoit2004}, equal risk contribution, hierarchical risk parity \cite{lopezdeprado2016} and minimum CVaR \cite{rockafellar2000}.

\emph{Expected.}
Third and fourth sample co-moments are noisy on 756 days.
I expected the global and local minimisers to coincide most of the time, and neither to be distinguishable from simple baselines \cite{demiguel2009}.

\emph{Observed.}
On U10 the certified minimiser equals the local one on every date.
On U37 the local solver is suboptimal by more than $10^{-4}$ in relative terms on \cSubZero{}, \cSubOne{} and \cSubThree{} of the dates, for $w_4=0,1,3$.
Every SOS solve is certified.

Out of sample the picture is flat (Table~\ref{tab:C}, Figure~\ref{fig:C}).
All \cTests{} net-return intervals contain zero.
The Sharpe-ratio test gives $p$ between \cPMin{} and \cPMax{} (Appendix~\ref{app:tests}, uncorrected).

On risk, the picture depends on the baseline.
With $w_4=1$ the U10 model has lower volatility than equal weights, by \cTenEwVol{} points (interval \cTenEwVolLo{} to \cTenEwVolHi{}).
It has higher volatility and CVaR than minimum variance and minimum CVaR.

On U37 the sector model is not better than the local solution of the same model.
Its volatility differs by \cSecLocVol{} points (\cSecLocVolLo{} to \cSecLocVolHi{}), so it is slightly higher.

The price of sparsity is large in model terms.
The full-model objective at the sector solution is \cSpSecOne{} on average against \cSpFullOne{} at the full-model local solution ($w_4=1$).
It is worse on \cSpWorseOne{} of the dates.

The SOS solve takes \cTimeTen{} s per rebalance on U10 and \cTimeSector{} s on U37, against \cTimeLocal{} s for the local solve of the sector model.
These are wall times with six concurrent workers and are indicative only.

\emph{Reading.}
The sparse global minimiser is better at the thing it optimises, and the gain does not transfer.
The most plausible reason is estimation error in the moments, which is what the protocol anticipated.
I did not separate it from model error.

\section{Discussion and limits}
What the experiments show.
On synthetic polynomials, local search fails often enough that a certificate has value, especially above ten assets.
The order-2 relaxation produces it in most cases, with a cost that exceeds 100 local runs at $n=50$.
On real data the certificate changes the allocation on few dates and does not improve on the risk-based baselines.

What they do not show.
They do not show that the certificate is useless for other objectives.
They do not show that the ball formulation fails for other uncertainty models, or with real estimation error instead of drawn error.
They do not show anything about markets beyond 37 fixed US stocks over one period.
The extrapolated dense costs at $n=30$ and $n=50$ are not measurements.

The bound-accuracy test covers validity and tightness at the $10^{-8}$ level on small non-convex instances with two or three assets.
It does not cover four assets or more, sparse relaxations with several cliques and auxiliary budget variables, or the real-data polynomials.
For those I rely on the re-solve sample of Appendix~\ref{app:acc}, which has no independent optimum, so a bias common to all solvers would go undetected.

Explanations I tested: the budget decoupling (fixed and confirmed by the regression test), and the solver accuracy behind the bound excess (Appendix~\ref{app:acc}).
Explanations I did not test: why local minima multiply with $n$, why shrinkage beats the ball in Experiment~B, and why the global minimiser does not transfer out of sample.
The synthetic polynomial has no return term and a simplified kurtosis structure, so Experiments~A and~B describe this family only.

I reviewed the results, ran an audit of the claims, and corrected the errors it found (Appendix~\ref{app:v1} and \texttt{docs/CLAIMS.md}).

\section{Accuracy of the bound}\label{app:acc}
In Experiment~C the bound exceeds the value of the same polynomial at the retained point, by a relative amount up to \xDefMaxU{}.
The sign is always the same, which first looked like an invalid relaxation.
I re-solved \xN{} windows with CLARABEL tolerance $10^{-9}$ instead of $10^{-7}$.
Where the solver converges (no weight on the fourth moment, and the 2020 windows of U37) the excess falls by a factor 30 to 100.
Where it stalls with an inaccurate status (most cases with $w_4\ge1$) the median ratio of the two excesses is 1.00, and the excess stays between $10^{-7}$ and $1.5\times10^{-6}$ in most cases.
The largest excess at tolerance $10^{-9}$ is \xTightMax{} and none is negative.
The polynomial is the same on both sides (checked to nine digits) and the retained points are feasible (largest violation $4\times10^{-16}$).
The reported value is the primal objective of the moment problem; the guaranteed bound would be the dual objective, which I did not extract.
On a two-asset instance with a known optimum the excess is $3.2\times10^{-9}$ and the solver residuals are about $10^{-9}$, at tolerance $10^{-7}$ and $10^{-10}$ alike.
I conclude that the excess is a precision floor of the solver, consistent with an unfinished solve, and not a flaw in the relaxation.

\paragraph{Sensitivity of the results.}
I re-solved a fixed sample of \spCases{} cases (seed 2026) with CLARABEL at $10^{-10}$ and SCS at $10^{-8}$ (Table~\ref{tab:F}).
The sample includes inaccurate statuses, uncertified instances and the tightest certificate gaps of each experiment.
Across the \spRuns{} re-solves, \spFlips{} certification verdicts change and \spFlagCh{} failure flags of Experiment~A change.
The SOS allocations of Experiment~C move by at most \spMaxW{}.

With CLARABEL, the bound itself changes by up to \spACl{} (A), \spBCl{} (B) and \spCCl{} (C, the 2020 windows of U37).
SCS differs by up to \spAScs{} on the 4-asset instances of A, where it also reports an inaccurate status.
Its flatness decisions differ from CLARABEL's in \spFlatFlips{} of \spFlatN{} cases of C, so I do not use it for rank decisions.
The \spDdN{} instances where the sparse bound is more than $10^{-3}$ below the dense one stay so with CLARABEL at $10^{-10}$.
The difference lies between \spDdMin{} and \spDdMax{} and changes by at most \spDdChange{}, so the weaker sparse bound is not a solver artefact.

I treat a change as harmless when it cannot alter a verdict or a figure of the paper.
Verdicts use a relative tolerance of $10^{-4}$.
The quantities that carry conclusions (verdicts, failure flags, allocations, dense-minus-sparse differences) change by at most \spConcl{} or not at all.
The bound values change by more than that on some cases, up to \spCCl{}, without altering any verdict.
I therefore did not rerun Experiments~A, B and~C.

\paragraph{Independent optimum.}
A test (\texttt{tests/test\_bound\_soundness.py}) compares the bound with a minimum computed without the SDP on \smN{} instances with two or three assets, by a fine grid followed by local refinement.
It also checks that the bound stays below $f$ at 2000 random feasible points, at the local solution and at the extracted points.
At tolerance $10^{-10}$ the difference between the bound and the independent minimum lies between \smMin{} and \smMax{}.
A tolerance of $10^{-9}$ cannot be enforced because the solver stalls with residuals of that order.
The test therefore allows $+10^{-8}$ (bound above the optimum) and $-5\times10^{-8}$ (bound far below it).
